What the cabinet says about a life

You do not collect the Residential Tenancies Act for fun. It went in because the rent was due. The Banking Act sits beside the fine print of an actual Westpac account; the seven superannuation files sit beside a QSuper booklet; five volumes of the Corporations Act sit beside the ideas that wanted a company to live in. Three Queensland transport acts cover the buses and ferries of a real commute, two police acts answer a real question about what an officer may do at the door, and a spreadsheet of United Nations treaties answers a bigger one: what has this country signed up to on my behalf?

So the cabinet is a portrait. Not of Australia's law, which is far larger, but of the slice of it that touched one Queenslander in 2012. The method for drawing your own portrait lives in the method, and the map from life domains to acts lives in your relevance.

The cabinet, measured

61 documents: acts, regulations, treaties, forms and fine print. More than one for every week of the year. First in: a small tax form, 19 November 2012. Last of the summer: the Queensland tenancy act, 5 January 2013.
~90 MB of law on disk, roughly the size of one album of MP3 files. Small enough to fit on the cheapest USB stick. It still took a summer to read.
~200× the gap between the heaviest file and the lightest: the Taxation Administration Act 1953 volume two at 5.5 MB, against the Superannuation (Leaving Australia Payments) Act 1990 at 28 KB. Both got read. Relevance set the order, not size.

A snapshot, not the current law

Every file here is the law as it stood when it was saved, mostly across the summer of 2012 to 2013. Acts get amended constantly, and several of these have been rewritten or replaced since. For today's versions, go to the registers themselves: the Federal Register of Legislation and the Queensland legislation site, both free. Nine registers maps the full set. And for advice about your own situation: a qualified lawyer, not a thirteen-year-old PDF.

Story highlights

The save-dates are a diary. Read in order, they show a summer spent working through the rules of an ordinary life, one drawer at a time.

  1. 19 Nov 2012

    The first form

    A small ATO form called Statement by a Supplier, saved twice in one morning. The question it raised: what else have I signed without reading? The cabinet opens.

  2. 25 Dec 2012

    Christmas with the Corporations Act

    All five volumes of the company rulebook filed at 5.38pm on Christmas Day, on a day that also took in the Banking Act, four maritime acts and both Queensland police acts. The police powers act went in late that night.

  3. 27 Dec 2012

    The Social Security sitting

    All five volumes of the Social Security Act 1991, about 16 MB of welfare law, saved in one sitting two days after Christmas, with the Fair Work Act and the Privacy Act alongside.

  4. 28 Dec 2012

    The super deep-dive

    Both volumes of the Superannuation Act 1976 and both volumes of the SIS Regulations, the detailed rules every super fund runs on, in a single day.

  5. 5 Jan 2013

    The last file of the summer

    The Queensland Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act. A renter finishing where the rent is paid. The summer read closes.

  6. 2014

    The cabinet stays open

    Four late additions: the G20 Brisbane concept paper in January, then the Patents Act and the Therapeutic Goods Act with its regulations in November, filed when the inventions and the health ideas needed them.

The opt-out drawer

The road tested, the road closed

Most filing cabinets have one drawer people ask about first. In this one: Magna Carta 1215, the Oaths Act 1867 (Queensland), the Bills of Exchange Act 1909, and an American form called the UCC-1 financing statement. These are the documents the opt-out forums lean on: the theory that with the right old charter and the right sworn words, a person can step outside the system altogether.

The honest record is that this road was tested. The documents went into the cabinet and were read at the source, next to the acts the theories cite. Read that way, the shine comes off. Magna Carta is a medieval settlement between a king and his barons, not a personal exit clause. The Oaths Act sets out how Queensland swears statements, and nothing more. The Bills of Exchange Act is plumbing for cheques and promissory notes. The UCC-1 is a filing form from a different country's commercial code.

The drawer stays in the cabinet because the dead end is part of the map. There is no out. There is only through: know the rules, meet them, and use the system's own tools to change what needs changing. That decision, made after this read, is what change the law is built on.
