Start from your own wallet, not from A.

Almost nobody reads the statute book front to back. You do not need to. The law that matters to you is already announcing itself: in your letterbox, on your payslip, inside your bank app. This room maps nine slices of an ordinary life to the acts behind them.

The alphabet is the wrong index

Every register of legislation sorts its acts the same way: by name, A to Z, and by year. That is the right index for a library and the wrong index for a life. There are thousands of acts across nine parliaments, with no single official count published, and no person is governed by all of them at once. Opening the register at A and reading forward is how the job stays undone.

The right index is sitting in your house. The letterbox, the payslip, the lease, the bank app, the medicine cabinet: each one is a physical pointer to a specific act (a law passed by a parliament) or to the regulations under it (subordinate legislation, the detailed rules made under an act). Law you can see in your own paperwork is law you will actually read. Law you cannot connect to anything you own or sign stays theoretical, and theoretical reading rarely lasts long.

That is the whole principle of this room, and of the method it feeds: relevance first. Gather the laws behind your own objects, and an afternoon's gathering gives you a working reading list.

One life, nine domains

Draw yourself in the middle. Around you, nine domains cover most of what statute law does to an ordinary week. Each spoke on this wheel leads to acts that were sitting in the 2012 cabinet, so every example below is a real file with a save-date, not a guess.

From everyday object to act

Each card below starts with a thing you can hold or tap, then names the law behind it. Every act named here is a real file from the 2012 cabinet; the full ledger has the sizes and save-dates.

MONEY

The bank app

Behind the login sits a product disclosure statement (PDS for short, the fine print booklet every account comes with), and behind the PDS sits the Banking Act 1959, which decides who may call themselves a bank and hold your deposits. The identity checks from the day you opened the account trace to the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006. The cabinet filed a Westpac PDS beside both.

TAX

The payslip

The tax that leaves your pay before you ever see it is PAYG withholding, set up by A New Tax System (Pay As You Go) Act 1999. The machinery of assessments, obligations and penalties is the Taxation Administration Act 1953, two volumes in the cabinet. Queensland runs its own parallel machinery under the Taxation Administration Act 2001 (QLD).

SUPER

The super statement

The annual statement your fund posts you is shaped by the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations 1994, the detailed rulebook every fund runs on, filed in two volumes on 28 December 2012, alongside the Superannuation Act 1976; the Superannuation Act 1990 had gone into the drawer three days earlier. The cabinet kept a QSuper PDS in the same drawer.

WORK

The job

The national workplace rulebook is the Fair Work Act 2009: minimum standards, awards (the industry-by-industry pay and conditions rulebooks), unfair dismissal. If you sit on the other side of the payroll, the Corporations Act 2001 governs the company itself; all five volumes went into the cabinet on Christmas Day 2012.

HOME

The lease

For a Queensland renter, the rights the agent does not read out loud are in the Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008 (QLD). It was the last file of the 2012 to 2013 summer read, saved 5 January 2013. Rent in another state and the same drawer exists on that state's register under a different name.

MOVING AROUND

The licence and the go card

The driver licence in your wallet answers to the Transport Operations (Road Use Management) Act 1995 (QLD): licences, fines, everything between you and the road. The bus or ferry you tap onto runs under the Transport Operations (Passenger Transport) Act 1994 (QLD), and the go card's parent body was constituted by the Transport Operations (TransLink Transit Authority) Act 2008 (QLD), as it stood in 2012.

HEALTH

The medicine cabinet

Everything in it that calls itself a medicine or a medical device is there because the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989 and its Regulations allow it to be sold as one. Both joined the cabinet in November 2014, when the health ideas needed them.

SPEECH AND DATA

The privacy notice

The privacy policy linked at the bottom of nearly every app points back to the Privacy Act 1988: what governments and companies may do with information about you. Its sibling in the cabinet is the Freedom of Information Act 1982, the right to see what the government holds about you. Both were read in 2012, well before the current wave of interest.

THE BALLOT

The ballot paper

The piece of paper you fill in every few years works the way it does because the Electoral Act 1992 (QLD) says so: how a Queensland election legally runs, start to finish. It is the entry point for anyone who wants to change the rules from inside. The federal version lives on the Federal Register of Legislation.

5.5 MB the heaviest single file in the cabinet is tax machinery: the Taxation Administration Act 1953, volume two. About the size of one sharp phone photo, holding a fair slice of the rules behind every Australian payslip.

Where each act lives: the Commonwealth ones are on the Federal Register of Legislation, the Queensland ones on Queensland Legislation, and AustLII holds free copies across all nine jurisdictions. The full tour of the registers is in nine registers.

The cabinet's filing habit

Notice the pattern in the ledger: the fine print gets filed beside the act it answers to. The bank's PDS sits next to the Banking Act 1959. The super fund's PDS sits next to the regulations that shape it. Two sides of the same drawer: what they gave you to sign, and what parliament wrote that made them give it to you. Reading them together is where the method starts to bite.

Your map will not be this map. A tradie's wheel is heavier on tools, vehicles and licensing. A parent's is heavier on family and school. A shop owner's pulls in leases of a different kind, food rules, employment from the employer side. That is the point: relevance is personal, and only you can draw your own wheel. The nine domains above are a starting frame, not a fence.
